AUSTRALIA'S URBAN WILDLIFE · 2016–2025

Wild Cities: Who Still Lives Among Us?

From birds above suburban streets to reptiles, mammals and thousands of insect species, Australia's cities support a surprising range of recorded wildlife.

This visualisation explores wildlife observations across Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Adelaide, Perth, Hobart, Darwin and Canberra–Queanbeyan.

Birds dominate the most frequently recorded species across all eight cities

Species are ranked by the number of cities with records,
with occurrence count breaking ties. All ten displayed species were recorded in eight cities, 2016–2025.

WHY ARE ALL TEN SPECIES BIRDS?

Across the eight urban areas, 10,643 distinct species were recorded during 2016–2025. However, only 121 were documented in all eight cities: 75 birds, 39 insects and 7 mammals.

These 121 species share the maximum geographic coverage of eight cities. Occurrence-record count therefore determines their ranking, with birds occupying all ten highest positions.

This reflects recording frequency rather than animal abundance or ecological dominance.

KEY FINDING

Australian Magpies and Rainbow Lorikeets lead the ranking, with over 540,000 occurrence records each.

Both species were recorded across all eight urban areas. Their high record counts reflect reporting frequency rather than necessarily larger populations.

HOW TO READ THIS

All ten species were recorded in eight cities. Bar length
therefore represents occurrence-record count, the secondary ranking criterion. Longer bars indicate more recorded observations, not greater abundance.

Insects dominate recorded species richness across all eight cities

Follow the four animal groups across eight urban areas. Ribbon thickness represents the number of distinct species recorded within each city–group combination, 2016–2025.

WHY DO INSECTS DOMINATE THE FLOWS?

Insects contribute substantially more recorded species richness than birds, mammals or reptiles, resulting in much wider ribbons. These flows represent documented species richness, not animal population sizes.

Species recorded in multiple cities contribute to multiple ribbons. Combined flow totals therefore represent summed city-level richness, rather than the number of unique species across all eight cities.

KEY FINDING

Insects account for the largest contribution to recorded species richness across the selected urban areas.

Their much wider flows highlight the unequal distribution of documented richness among animal groups. The smaller bird, mammal and reptile flows remain part of the same quantitative comparison.

HOW TO READ THIS

Each coloured ribbon connects an animal group to an urban area. Ribbon thickness represents the number of distinct species recorded for that city–group combination. Wider ribbons indicate greater recorded richness. Hover over a ribbon to view exact values. Combined flow totals count species separately in each city where they were recorded.

CHANGE THROUGH TIME

Recorded urban wildlife does not stand still.

Brisbane, Melbourne and Sydney trade the lead in recorded wildlife richness

Annual rankings show how recorded species richness shifted among eight urban areas from 2016–2025.

WHO HELD THE LEAD?

Melbourne led in 2016–2017, Brisbane in 2018–2019 and Sydney in 2020–2021. Brisbane returned to first place in 2022–2023, Melbourne briefly regained the lead in 2024, and Brisbane finished first in 2025.

These movements show changes in cities' relative rankings for recorded species richness. They do not necessarily represent increases or declines in underlying biodiversity.

KEY FINDING

No city held first place throughout the decade. Brisbane,
Melbourne and Sydney alternated at the top of the ranking.

Brisbane rose from #3 in 2016 to #1 in 2025, while
Canberra–Queanbeyan remained consistently fourth. Lower-ranked cities were comparatively stable, with Adelaide and Perth exchanging positions and Darwin and Hobart alternating between seventh and eighth.

HOW TO READ THIS

Each line represents one urban area. Rank 1 indicates the
highest recorded species richness for that year. A line moving
upward means the city ranked higher relative to the other
seven urban areas; it does not show the magnitude of
the richness difference.

04 CONNECTED CITIES

City boundaries do not define wildlife communities.

Geographic layouts can suffer from severe overlapping, hiding the true strength of the network. This circular layout reorganises the cities by recorded richness to cleanly expose the relationships.

Australian cities share recorded wildlife species, but the degree of overlap varies

Explore shared wildlife records across eight Australian urban areas from 2016–2025. Thicker connections indicate more distinct species documented in both connected cities.

KEY FINDING

Recorded wildlife overlap varies substantially between city pairs.

Thicker connections indicate more shared recorded species, highlighting where documented wildlife distributions overlap most strongly in absolute terms. These connections describe shared recorded species, not animal movement between cities. Observation effort may also influence the measured overlap.

HOW TO READ THIS

Each circle represents one urban area. Circle area shows total recorded species richness, and colour identifies the city. Each grey line connects a pair of cities. Thicker lines represent more distinct species recorded in both cities. The circular layout is schematic. Node positions and line lengths do not represent geographic distances.

About the data

Wildlife occurrence records were sourced from the Atlas of Living Australia and spatially restricted to eight ABS Urban Centres and Localities. Threat status was matched against current EPBC threatened fauna listings from DCCEEW.

Data sources: Atlas of Living Australia · Australian Bureau of Statistics · Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water.
