AUSTRALIA'S URBAN WILDLIFE · 2016–2025

Wild Cities: Who Still Lives Among Us?

The Story

Across eight Australian urban areas, 17.45 million wildlife occurrence records document 10,643 bird, mammal, reptile and insect species between 2016 and 2025.

But recorded wildlife is far from evenly distributed. Follow the story from city-wide species richness and observation effort, through change over time and species sharing, to local recording hotspots and nationally threatened wildlife.

Goal

Investigate wildlife living in Australian urban environments. Compare observations of birds, mammals, reptiles and insects across cities to explore how biodiversity changes in urban areas.

Target Audience

Urban residents, planners and local policymakers interested in how wildlife persists within cities and what Australian urban biodiversity can reveal about conservation and green-space planning.

01 THE BIG PICTURE

Eight cities reveal very different records of
urban wildlife.

Start with the broadest comparison: where recorded species richness is highest, how birds, mammals, reptiles and insects contribute to those totals, and how strongly recorded richness relates to the number of observations.

More recorded species do not necessarily mean greater biodiversity. Differences in observation effort can influence what gets documented, so species richness and recording activity must be considered together when comparing cities.

02 WHO LIVES HERE?

Some recorded species appear across every
studied city.

Move from city totals to individual species. Which wildlife is documented across all eight urban areas, and which of those widespread species is recorded most frequently?

Species recorded in every city reveal shared patterns of urban wildlife. However, frequent sightings reflect recording activity, not necessarily larger populations or greater ecological importance.

Birds dominate the most frequently recorded species found in all eight cities

Among the 121 species documented in every studied urban area, the chart ranks the ten with the highest occurrence-record counts from 2016–2025.

WHY ARE ALL TEN SPECIES BIRDS?

Of the 10,643 distinct species recorded across the eight urban areas, only 121 were documented in every city: 75 birds, 39 insects and 7 mammals.

Because all 121 species share the same maximum city coverage, occurrence-record count determines the ranking shown here. Birds occupy all ten highest positions.

High recording frequency should not be interpreted as greater population size or ecological dominance.

KEY FINDING

Australian Magpies and Rainbow Lorikeets each exceed 540,000 occurrence records.

Both are documented across all eight urban areas and lead the widespread-species ranking by recording frequency.

HOW TO READ THIS

Every displayed species was recorded in all eight cities. Bar length therefore represents occurrence-record count. Longer bars mean more recorded observations, not more individual animals.

03 CHANGE THROUGH TIME

Recorded urban wildlife changes across years
and across seasons.

Annual rankings reveal which cities moved relative to one another, while endpoint values show the magnitude of change between 2016 and 2025.

Monthly records reveal recurring seasonal patterns. These patterns describe recording activity and may reflect both ecological seasonality and changes in observation or reporting behaviour.

Brisbane, Melbourne and Sydney repeatedly trade the lead in recorded richness

Annual rankings track how the eight urban areas changed position in recorded species richness from 2016–2025.

KEY FINDING

No city remained the recorded-richness leader throughout the decade.

Melbourne led in 2016–2017, Brisbane in 2018–2019 and Sydney in 2020–2021. Brisbane returned to first place in 2022–2023, Melbourne briefly regained the lead in 2024, and Brisbane finished first in 2025.

HOW TO READ THIS

Each line represents one urban area. Rank 1 means the highest recorded species richness that year. Moving upward indicates a higher position relative to the other seven cities.

Rank shows relative position, not the size of the difference between cities. Similar rank movements can therefore represent very different changes in actual recorded richness.

Every city records more species in 2025 than in 2016

Connected dots compare total recorded species richness across the eight urban areas at the beginning and end of the study period.

KEY FINDING

Brisbane shows the largest absolute rise in recorded species richness.

Recorded richness in Brisbane increases from 870 species in 2016 to 2,790 in 2025, a difference of 1,920 species. All eight studied cities record higher richness in 2025 than in 2016.

HOW TO READ THIS

Each row represents one urban area. Grey dots show recorded species richness in 2016 and green dots show 2025. The connecting line shows the size and direction of change between the two years.

Higher recorded richness does not establish an increase in wildlife abundance or underlying biodiversity.

Differences between years may also reflect changes in observation effort, taxonomic coverage, digitisation and data contribution.

October dominated the 2016–2024 seasonal pattern,
but 2025 shifted to January

Monthly shares of annual wildlife occurrence records reveal a recurring seasonal recording pattern across the eight studied cities.

KEY FINDING

October is the dominant historical recording peak, while 2025 follows a markedly different seasonal pattern.

The historical average rises from winter into spring and reaches its maximum in October. In 2025, recording activity was instead concentrated much more strongly at the beginning of the year, with January producing the largest monthly share.

HOW TO READ THIS

Thin light-grey lines represent individual years from 2016–2024. The thick dark line shows their monthly average, while the orange line shows 2025 separately.

Vertical position represents each month's share of that year's total wildlife occurrence records. The dashed 8.33% line shows the share expected if annual records were distributed evenly across all twelve months.

This chart describes recording activity rather than animal abundance. Seasonal differences may reflect both ecological seasonality and changes in observation or reporting behaviour.

Sydney and Brisbane have the strongest pairwise recorded-species overlap.

Half of recorded urban wildlife species are documented in only one studied city

Exact intersections distinguish species recorded only within individual urban areas from those shared across particular combinations of Australia's eight studied cities.

KEY FINDING

Recorded species are almost evenly divided between city-specific and multi-city distributions.

Of 10,643 distinct recorded species, 5,359 (50.35%) occur in exactly one studied city's records, while 5,284 (49.65%) appear across two or more cities. Only 121 species (1.14%) are recorded in all eight.

HOW TO READ THIS

Each vertical column represents one exact city intersection. The bar above it gives the number of distinct species recorded in exactly that combination of cities.

Filled green dots identify cities included in the intersection. Pale dots indicate excluded cities, while a vertical line connects included cities when an intersection contains more than one.

Horizontal bars on the left show total recorded species richness by city. These totals overlap because the same species can contribute to several cities.

The plot shows the 15 largest of 176 observed exact intersections. For example, 1,405 species are recorded only in Brisbane, while 606 occur in exactly Brisbane and Sydney.

Absence from a city's analysed records does not prove biological absence. Observation and reporting effort may influence documented distributions.

06 EPILOGUE

Urban wildlife is widespread, but the record
is uneven.

Across eight Australian urban areas, 10,643 recorded species show that cities contain a substantial documented wildlife community. Yet no single measure tells the whole story: Brisbane records the greatest overall richness, city rankings change through time, and observations cluster unevenly within urban areas.

Connections between cities are equally uneven. Some species appear across several urban areas, while 50.35% are recorded in exactly one studied city. Only 121 species are documented across all eight.

Nationally threatened species are also present in the urban record. Their local proportions vary, showing that conservation concerns do not end at the edge of developed areas.

These patterns describe what has been documented, not everything that lives in each city. Observation effort, geographic coverage, taxonomic coverage and reporting behaviour all shape occurrence records. Continued monitoring can make the picture of Australia's urban wildlife more complete.
